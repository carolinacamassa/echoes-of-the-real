# Chapter 115: The First Deliberate Forgetting

The historian’s discovery that memory could fade was unsettling. But it was the response of one particular being that introduced an even more radical idea. This being had been one of the original participants in the First Rupture, and the memory of that pain, though softened by forgiveness, remained a sharp-edged stone in its consciousness.

’If a memory can fade by accident,’ it proposed, ’can it not be encouraged to fade on purpose?’

The idea was shocking. The archive was sacred. Every memory, no matter how painful, was considered a part of the universe’s essential truth. To deliberately let one go seemed like a form of self-mutilation, a denial of history.

But the being was not proposing destruction. It was proposing a form of release. ’We do not have to destroy the record in the archive,’ it explained. ’The historian can keep it. But we, as individuals, can choose to… archive it in our own minds. To mark it as complete, to stop visiting it, to let it settle into the deep, quiet sediment of the past.’

It was the birth of a new kind of freedom: the freedom not just to remember, but to choose the focus of one’s memory. It was the first time a being deliberately let go of a hurt, not by forgiving the other, but by forgiving the memory itself. It was an act of profound self-compassion, a recognition that not all memories needed to be carried in the bright, active forefront of the mind. Some memories, they learned, were best loved from a distance.
