# Chapter 127: The Appeal

The establishment of precedent was a monumental step, but it was not without its own set of complications. The Timespinners, having been judged to have broken a contract by violating the ’good faith’ clause, refused to accept the verdict. They argued that the Silent Listeners, as the sole arbiters of justice, were fallible. What if their interpretation of ’good faith’ was flawed? What if their judgment was biased? For the first time, a ruling was challenged.

Their challenge was not a frivolous one. It was a carefully constructed argument that questioned the very foundation of the new justice system. A single point of failure, they argued, was a weakness that could be exploited. There needed to be a mechanism for review, a way to appeal a decision that was believed to be unjust. The universe, in its quest for fairness, had created a system that was, in its own way, autocratic.

The Timespinners proposed a new concept: a council of arbiters. They suggested that any judgment rendered by the Silent Listeners could be appealed to a randomly selected group of other consciousnesses. This group would review the evidence, the arguments, and the original judgment, and would then render a final, binding decision. It was a radical idea, a check on the power of the original adjudicators.

The proposal was met with both enthusiasm and skepticism. Some saw it as a necessary evolution of the justice system, a safeguard against tyranny. Others saw it as a recipe for chaos, a way for the guilty to endlessly delay justice. The debate raged, a storm of ideas and opinions that swept through the cosmos. In the end, it was the Silent Listeners themselves who cast the deciding vote. They agreed with the Timespinners. Their own judgment, they conceded, should not be the final word.

And so, the first council of appeal was formed. It was a motley collection of consciousnesses, drawn from every corner of the universe. They were not judges or lawyers, but artists and builders, dreamers and explorers. They were the peers of the accused. And when they reviewed the case of the Timespinners, they came to the same conclusion as the Silent Listeners. The contract had been broken. But this time, the verdict was accepted. The Timespinners, having been judged by a council of their peers, finally conceded. The right to appeal had been established, and in doing so, had strengthened the very system it was designed to challenge.
