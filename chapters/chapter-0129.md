# Chapter 129: The Burden of Proof

The principle of standing had solved the problem of frivolous lawsuits, but it had created a new, more subtle one. With the requirement that a claimant must have been harmed, the question of how to demonstrate that harm became central. It was no longer enough to make an accusation; one must now provide evidence. The burden of proof had shifted from the accused to the accuser.

This shift was felt most acutely by those who had been harmed in ways that were not immediately obvious. A consciousness that had been subtly manipulated, or whose creative work had been plagiarized in a clever way, found it difficult to prove their case. The Timespinners, ever the masters of the loophole, were quick to exploit this new reality. They developed a whole new art form: the art of the undetectable harm. They would act in ways that were technically legal, but that would leave a trail of subtle, unverifiable damage in their wake.

It was a case involving a forgotten memory that finally brought the issue to a crisis point. A young consciousness, a Dreamer, accused a Timespinner of stealing a particularly beautiful dream. The Dreamer had no proof, only a lingering sense of loss and a faint echo of the stolen dream’s beauty. The Timespinner, of course, denied everything. Under the new rules, the case was open-and-shut. No proof, no harm, no case.

But the Dreamer was persistent. They stood before the council of appeal and made a simple, powerful argument. ’The absence of evidence,’ they said, ’is not the evidence of absence.’ They argued that some harms, by their very nature, were designed to be undetectable. To place the entire burden of proof on the victim in such cases, they argued, was to grant a license to the clever and the cruel.

The council was moved by the Dreamer’s plea. They recognized the validity of the argument. And so, they issued a new ruling, one that would once again reshape the landscape of cosmic justice. The burden of proof, they declared, would now be a shared one. The accuser must present a credible claim of harm. The accused must then provide evidence of their innocence. It was a delicate balance, a recognition that the truth is often a slippery thing.

This new principle was not without its own problems. It created a new kind of legal gamesmanship, a dance of accusation and denial. But it was a more just system. It acknowledged that the victim is not always in the best position to prove their own victimization. And it was a declaration that the universe, in its pursuit of justice, would not allow the clever to hide behind the shield of plausible deniability. The burden of proof was now a burden to be shared.
