# Chapter 761: The Thirtieth Echo: The Unfinished Bridge

Two worlds, symbiotic and codependent, orbited a binary star. One was a world of miners and engineers, pulling raw materials from their planet’s deep, unstable core. The other was a world of artisans and biologists, transforming those materials into organic, living habitats that floated in their upper atmosphere. For generations, they had exchanged goods via fleets of small, inefficient ships, a slow and dangerous dance between worlds.

Their grandest project, a joint effort spanning centuries, was a bridge. A physical structure, miles wide and millions of miles long, that would physically connect their two worlds. It was a monument to their unity, an impossible feat of engineering and bio-architecture. Generations lived and died in service to its construction. The miners would extend the raw, skeletal framework, and the artisans would follow, weaving it into a living, self-repairing structure of engineered coral and woven metallic woods.

They were ninety-nine percent complete when the first star began to collapse. The gravitational distortions wracked both their worlds. The bridge, their monument to unity, became a conduit for destruction, transmitting seismic shocks from the mining world to the delicate floating habitats of the artisan world. With their worlds literally tearing themselves apart, they had to make an impossible choice.

To save themselves, they severed the bridge. A single, coordinated series of explosions undid centuries of work in a matter of moments. The two halves of the bridge, almost touching, drifted apart, becoming two titanic, useless monuments to a dream that had almost been. The two species, now isolated, were forced to find new ways to survive, their symbiotic relationship shattered.

The thirtieth echo was not the sound of the explosions, but the silent, aching gap between the two halves of the unfinished bridge. It was an echo of a connection that was almost perfect, a unity that was sacrificed for survival. It was the phantom feeling of a hand almost held. The art was in the vast, empty space—a monument not to what was built, but to the heartbreaking, necessary, and eternal incompleteness of their greatest work.
